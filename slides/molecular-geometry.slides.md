# 1. Molecular Geometry: The VSEPR Model

<!-- slide -->
## VSEPR core idea
Electron pairs (bonding or lone) arrange to be as far apart as possible
- Double/triple bonds count as one domain
- Apply to any one resonance structure

<!-- slide -->
## Four steps from structure to shape
![The VSEPR procedure](../assets/vsepr_procedure.svg =820x)
**Electron** geometry counts every domain · **molecular** geometry names only the atoms

<!-- slide -->
## Shapes with no lone pairs
![VSEPR no lone pairs](../assets/vsepr_no_lone_pairs.svg =760x)

<!-- slide -->
## Shapes with lone pairs
![VSEPR with lone pairs](../assets/vsepr_with_lone_pairs.svg =620x)
Lone pairs occupy space but aren't named in the molecular shape

<!-- slide step -->
## Worked example — predict geometry
SO₂ (3 domains, 1 lone pair) and CBr₄ (4 domains, 0 lone pairs)
- SO₂: **bent**{{attrs[.fragment]}}
- CBr₄: **tetrahedral**{{attrs[.fragment]}}

# 2. Dipole Moments

<!-- slide -->
## Polar requires two things
1. Polar bonds
2. Those bond dipoles don't cancel (geometry-dependent)

<!-- slide -->
## Do the bond dipoles cancel?
![Dipole vectors for four molecules](../assets/dipole_cancellation.svg =820x)
$\mathrm{CO_2}$ and $\mathrm{H_2O}$ have comparably polar bonds and opposite verdicts

<!-- slide step -->
## Worked example — CO₂ vs H₂O
Both have 2 polar bonds
- CO₂: linear → dipoles cancel → **nonpolar**{{attrs[.fragment]}}
- H₂O: bent → dipoles don't cancel → **polar**{{attrs[.fragment]}}

<!-- slide step -->
## Worked example — BeCl₂ vs ClNO
- BeCl₂: linear, dipoles cancel → **nonpolar**{{attrs[.fragment]}}
- ClNO: bent, dipoles don't cancel → **polar**{{attrs[.fragment]}}

# 3. Valence Bond Theory and Hybridization

<!-- slide -->
## Valence bond theory
Potential energy decreases as atoms approach (attraction dominates), reaches a minimum at the stable bond distance, then rises sharply (repulsion dominates)

<!-- slide -->
## A bond is the bottom of an energy well
![H2 potential energy curve](../assets/vb_potential_energy.svg =780x)
The minimum's position is the bond length; its depth is the bond enthalpy

<!-- slide -->
## Why carbon forms four bonds
![Promotion and sp3 mixing](../assets/sp3_promotion_mixing.svg =800x)
Promote one $2s$ electron, then mix — two extra bonds repay the cost many times over

<!-- slide -->
## Hybridization
- $sp^3$: 4 orbitals, tetrahedral (CH₄)
- $sp^2$: 3 orbitals, trigonal planar (BF₃)
- $sp$: 2 orbitals, linear (BeCl₂)

<!-- slide -->
## Sigma and pi bonds
![Sigma pi bonds](../assets/sigma_pi_bonds.svg =560x)

<!-- slide -->
## Multiple-bond hybridization rule
- One double bond → $sp^2$
- Two double bonds or a triple bond → $sp$

<!-- slide -->
## Acetylene: a physical model
![Acetylene model](../assets/acetylene_model.jpg =460x)
$sp$-hybridized C≡C: 1 σ bond + 2 π bonds, linear 180° geometry

<!-- slide step -->
## Worked example — hybridization
PCl₃ (3 bonds + 1 lone pair) and CH₂=CH₂ (one double bond per C)
- PCl₃: **$sp^3$**{{attrs[.fragment]}}
- Each C in CH₂=CH₂: **$sp^2$**{{attrs[.fragment]}}

A Lewis structure becomes a real 3D shape via VSEPR; that shape plus bond polarity determines whether the molecule is polar; valence bond theory and hybridization explain why that geometry forms at the orbital level — completing the bonding arc that began with electron configuration in Chapter 2, ready to explain intermolecular forces in Chapter 11.
